# 25 August 2026

## An index that could grow but never shrink

Reported: an account showing **21,538 indexed** for a mailbox whose inbox and
trash had both been emptied — and pressing "Index now" repeatedly changed
nothing. The same on every account.

It could not have. Two mechanisms fill the index and neither can empty it:

- **A backfill only adds.** It pages through the mailbox writing rows. Mail
  deleted while it is still running is simply never seen, and nothing goes
  back for it.
- **An incremental pass only applies what `history.list` reports since its
  cursor.** Anything deleted before that cursor existed, or after it lapsed,
  is invisible to it — and Gmail keeps only about thirty days of history.

So the index had no way to notice, and — the part that made it feel broken —
**no way to be rebuilt**. "Index now" advances the index; it has never been a
re-index, and from the outside those two are indistinguishable.

Three changes:

**A real rebuild.** `POST /accounts/:id/reindex` throws the index away and
starts a fresh backfill, behind a confirmation that says what it costs:
nothing in Gmail changes, but searching falls back to asking Gmail directly
until it finishes.

**A drift warning.** The index cannot see its own staleness, but the two
counts can: the number of rows against the mailbox's own `messagesTotal`. More
than a tenth over and the card says the index is out of date and offers the
rebuild. A tenth of slack because the numbers are taken at different moments
and Gmail counts drafts and chats in its own total.

**The mailbox size is refreshed every pass**, not only at backfill time — one
call, and it is what makes that comparison honest rather than a snapshot from
whenever the account was first indexed.

## The rebuild happens on its own now

Asked for: rebuild automatically, every six hours.

Taken as *check* every six hours rather than *rebuild* every six hours, and
worth saying why. A rebuild reads the entire mailbox again — real Gmail quota,
and search degraded to asking Gmail directly until it finishes. Nineteen
mailboxes rebuilt four times a day would cost far more than the drift it
fixes, permanently, for a problem that occurs occasionally.

So the sweep checks on every pass and rebuilds only when the index is holding
more than the mailbox does — more than a tenth over its `messagesTotal` — and
at most once every six hours per account. The tenth of slack is because the
two numbers are read at different moments and Gmail counts drafts and chats in
its own total; the six hours is what stops a wrong guess doing it repeatedly.
`sync_state.last_rebuild_at` records it, whoever triggered it.

The card says so, so an index that rebuilds itself is not a mystery: the
warning now reads "Hive rebuilds it on its own within a few hours; the button
does it now."

The decision rules have no Playwright reach — they are server-side and the
suite stubs at the API boundary — so they were exercised directly: badly
drifted, drifted but rebuilt an hour ago, drifted and rebuilt seven hours ago,
healthy, 5% over, 11% over, still backfilling, and no known mailbox size.

## The drift warning was unfixable, and it was mine

Reported an hour after shipping: some accounts still say "This index is out of
date" after repeated rebuilds.

They always would have. The check compared the number of indexed rows against
the mailbox's `messagesTotal` — and those two count different things. The
index deliberately holds Spam and Trash (I added that yesterday, to fix Spam
showing empty); `messagesTotal` does not. So any account with a full bin was
permanently over the threshold: the warning survived every rebuild, and the
automatic rebuild I added on top ran again six hours later, for ever. A
heuristic that cannot settle is worse than no heuristic, and this one was
expensive as well as wrong.

Replaced with a comparison that cannot be wrong in that way: **Gmail's own
list of message ids against ours**. Both sides are the same question asked of
the same mailbox, so there is nothing to calibrate and no threshold to guess.
Anything indexed that Gmail no longer lists is deleted outright.

It is also far cheaper than what it replaces. Ids come back 500 to a call and
carry no metadata, so checking a twenty-thousand-message mailbox is about
forty cheap requests — against twenty thousand expensive ones to rebuild it.
Reconciliation runs every six hours per account; **Rebuild** stays as the way
to do it now rather than wait.

The warning is gone, because there is no longer a state it describes: the
index repairs itself in passing.

The set arithmetic behind it was exercised directly — nothing deleted, most
deleted, everything deleted, an empty index, and a mailbox holding mail we
have not indexed yet, which must remove nothing.

## Searching left the folder you were standing in

Reported as "Sent shows the wrong data" — and the folder itself was fine. The
problem appeared only while searching, because a search deliberately widened
to the whole mailbox.

That was my decision, and it was defensible: searching the inbox used to miss
archived mail, which is a real trap. But it traded one surprise for another.
Standing in Sent and typing a word returned inbound mail, because "everywhere"
means exactly that — and the folder on screen stopped meaning anything the
moment a word was typed. The same in Drafts, and in every other destination.

The default is flipped: a search stays in the folder it was made from. That
folder is the one the person chose, which makes it the more defensible
default. **Search everywhere** is one click away, the header says which reach
is in force either way, and the empty state points at the wider search rather
than leaving someone to guess.

Viewing a sender from the analysis still reaches everything, on purpose: the
analysis counted the mailbox, so showing only this folder's share of it would
be a smaller answer than the number that was clicked.

## The sender list was capped at two hundred

Two hundred was chosen when the list was the only way to read it. There is a
search over it now, and cutting a mailbox's senders at two hundred hides
exactly the long tail someone is hunting through it for. The ceiling is five
thousand, and when it bites the panel says so rather than presenting a slice
as the whole — the result is stored as one row and rendered as one list, so
"all of them" cannot be unbounded.

## The sender list: no cap, a hundred at a time

The cap is gone entirely — it was two hundred this morning and five thousand
an hour ago, and both hid the long tail the list is most often opened to find.
A run returns every distinct sender.

The list is scanned rather than read, so the panel draws **a hundred rows** and
keeps the rest behind *Load more*. That is a render limit and nothing else,
which is the part worth being careful about: everything that acts on the list
acts on **all** of it. **Select all** ticks every sender, not the hundred
drawn, and the search runs across all of them — searching for a sender sitting
at position 240 finds it without loading anything first. The count beside the
selection is what makes that visible rather than surprising.

The drawn count resets to the first screenful whenever the list is re-cut — a
new run, a different mailbox chip, one of the three totals, or a change to the
search — because a list left scrolled open would otherwise show a stale amount
of a different set.

One real bound remains and it is not arbitrary: a run is stored as a single
row, so an enormous result may fail to save. That is already caught and
logged, and it costs a cached copy rather than the answer itself.

## Two cards, one page, two different widths

**Order.** The index draws a row per connected mailbox, and nineteen of those
pushed the cleanup rules — the thing the page is named after — off the bottom
of the screen. Rules first, index below it.

**Width.** The cards were 672px and 832px. Two causes, and the second is the
one worth remembering:

- The index card had no width of its own, so it was sized by its widest row —
  an address plus three buttons — and stretched past the page. The rules card
  sat at 42rem, the measure meant for a form.
- The rule written to fix that did nothing, because **there are three `.view`
  rules in this stylesheet** — 46rem, `none`, and 52rem — and the last one
  wins wherever it lands. A wide-page rule written above them is not a rule at
  all. It lives at the end of the file now, with a note saying why.

Both cards are 60rem and identical. The test asserts the order and that every
card on the page reports the same width, rather than asserting a number —
which is the actual complaint, and survives a change of measure.
